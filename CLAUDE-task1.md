# CLAUDE.md — IELTS Writing Task 1

Reference and working instructions for Task 1, built from *Cambridge English Mindset for IELTS* 1, 2 and 3. Every phrase marked **[M]** is printed in those books; everything else is added to fill gaps the series leaves open.

---

## Complexity scale used throughout

| Rating | What it signals | Where it lands a candidate |
|---|---|---|
| **B1** | Correct, safe, structurally flat. | Band 5–6. Use once per answer at most. |
| **B2** | The working register of a competent answer. | Band 6.5–7. This should be the bulk of the script. |
| **C1** | Nominalisation, participle clauses, clefts, apposition. | Band 7.5+. Three or four well-placed instances is enough. |
| **⚠ Risk** | High value but commonly misused, or wrong register. | Can cost more than it gains. |

A script made entirely of C1 phrases reads as memorised and is penalised under Lexical Resource. The target shape is a B2 spine with C1 at the overview, at one body hinge, and at the summary.

---

## Rules that hold across every Task 1 type

**Tone.** Formal, impersonal, scientific. Mindset Level 3 Unit 1 fails a sample answer explicitly for sounding "like a magazine article." No *I*, no *we*, no *you*, no rhetorical questions (*So, what does it all mean?* is marked down in that same exercise), no contractions.

**The five exclusions.** Never: speculate about *why* data changed; give an opinion; import outside knowledge; list every figure; copy the question's wording into the introduction. Mindset tests each of these separately — Level 1 Unit 4 makes students find the two sentences in a model that break the outside-information rule.

**Subjective adverbs are banned.** Acceptable: *gradually, markedly, noticeably, progressively, sharply, significantly, steadily*. Banned because they interpret rather than report: *amazingly, shockingly, surprisingly, predictably, worryingly*. Level 3 Unit 5 extends this to linkers — *Obviously* implies the reader already knows, *Surprisingly* implies outside knowledge, *At last* implies relief.

**Never passivise these verbs.** *rise, fall, increase, decrease, grow, occur, remain, change, expand, double, decline, drop, plummet, rocket, peak.* They are intransitive in this sense. ✗ *has been increased by 20%* ✗ *was almost doubled* ✗ *was occurred*. The series drills this error three separate times, which tells you how common it is.

**Word count.** 150 minimum. Under 150 is an automatic Task Achievement penalty.

# TYPE 1 — PROCESS DIAGRAM

*Mindset: L1 U1 (stringhoppers), L2 U3 (lithography, news story), L3 U8 (Wesak lanterns, wooden clogs)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Present simple throughout. No exceptions, whatever the topic. |
| **Voice** | Present simple passive as the **default**, not the rule. Both Mindset models run at roughly 55% passive. |
| **Tone** | Formal, technical, impersonal. |
| **Grammar load** | Passive transformation; modal passives; passive infinitives; sequencing adverbials; relative clauses defining equipment. |
| **Structure** | Introduction → overview (may sit at the start **or** the end) → body covering every stage but not every minor detail. |
| **No comparison** | Unlike data tasks, nothing is being compared. Do not force comparative language in. |

**Where the active belongs.** Three slots, and only three:
1. The framing sentence — *The diagram shows… / The process consists of six stages.*
2. Stage-naming nominals — *The first stage is to obtain wood… / The final task is to decorate…* These carry the sequencing and break up a wall of passives.
3. Clauses where the agent is meaningful — *the clog maker takes a long knife to cut the shoe to shape.* **[M]**

### Introduction

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| The diagram shows the process of making X. | B1 | The weakest reporting verb. Correct, invisible, scores nothing. |
| The diagram illustrates how X is made. **[M]** | B2 | *Illustrates* is a genuine upgrade, and the embedded passive clause adds a second clause free. |
| Each picture represents a stage in the process of… **[M]** | B2 | Useful when the diagram is a strip of images rather than a flow chart. |
| The diagram demonstrates the process of preparing stringhoppers, **a kind of noodle**. **[M]** | B2+ | The appositive gloss is the cheapest C1 trick in Task 1: define an unfamiliar noun in a comma-bounded phrase. Works on any unfamiliar term the diagram supplies. |
| The flow chart sets out the stages involved in the production of X. | C1 | *Sets out* + the nominalisation *the production of*. Precise and formal. |
| The diagram depicts the procedure by which X is manufactured. | C1 ⚠ | *Depicts* leans pictorial — natural for maps, faintly odd for a technical flow chart. *By which* is high-value but frequently mangled. |

### Overview

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| There are six stages in the process. | B1 | Counting is the minimum viable overview. |
| There are six main stages, beginning with X and ending **in** Y. **[M]** | B2 | Mindset prints *ending in*; *ending with* is the commoner collocation and safer. |
| The process comprises eight distinct stages, from the initial X through to the final Y. | C1 | *Comprises* takes no *of* — ✗ *comprises of* is a frequent error. *From… through to* outperforms *from… to*. |
| Overall, the process is entirely manual, requiring no specialist machinery. **[M]** | C1 | The strongest kind of process overview: it **characterises** the process instead of counting it. A present-participle clause does the work. |
| In conclusion, this simple process shows how… **[M]** | B2 | Mindset's closing template. Serviceable; *To conclude* is interchangeable. |

### Sequencing the body

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| First, / Then, / Next, / After that, / Finally, **[M]** | B1 | The complete Level 1 set. Necessary scaffolding; do not build the whole answer from it. |
| The next stage is to… / At this stage… / Once this is done… **[M]** | B2 | Active, and it breaks a run of passives without losing register. |
| Following this, / Subsequently, / Thereafter, | B2+ | *Subsequently* is the safest formal substitute for *then*. |
| Having been dried, the leaves are then packed. | C1 | Perfect passive participle. One per script is plenty — two reads as showing off. |
| …before being transported to the processing plant. **[M]** | C1 | Passive gerund after a preposition. Compresses a whole stage into a phrase. |
| Whilst this is happening, / At the same time, / Simultaneously, **[M]** | B2+ | **The most under-taught language in Task 1.** Only Level 2 Unit 3 supplies simultaneity markers. Most candidates write every process as purely linear even when the diagram shows parallel stages. |

### Passive forms, graded

| Form | Example | Rating |
|---|---|---|
| Present simple passive | The rice **is put** into a grinder. **[M]** | B1 |
| Passive + agent-free instrument | The wood **is cut** into pieces **using a saw**. **[M]** | B2 |
| Modal passive | Four squares **have to be tied** together. **[M]** | B2+ |
| Passive infinitive | The lantern is ready **to be decorated**. **[M]** | B2+ |
| Reduced passive relative | the techniques **used** for carving wooden shoes **[M]** | B2+ |
| Passive gerund | before **being printed** onto paper **[M]** | C1 |

# TYPE 2 — MAPS AND PLANS

*Mindset: L1 U2 (Sunnyhills campus, Colwick Arts Centre), L2 U4 (Mumbai, Dubai), L3 U3 (Poulton, beach hotel)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | **Governed entirely by the dates on the maps.** See the decision rule below. |
| **Voice** | Roughly 75% active. Passive only for construction, demolition and relocation where the agent is unknown. |
| **Tone** | Formal, descriptive, spatial. |
| **Grammar load** | Prepositions of place; change verbs and their nominalisations; past/present perfect contrast; passive for agentless change. |
| **Structure** | Introduction → overview → what changed → what stayed the same → summary. |

### The tense decision rule

| What the second map is labelled | Tense set | Mindset source |
|---|---|---|
| A past date (e.g. 1935) | Past simple for the first map, **past perfect** for the later one | L3 U3: *by 1935 this **had been removed*** |
| *Now* / *today* | **Present perfect** for the change + present simple for the current state | L2 U4: *A motorway **has been built**… The church and pond still **remain** the same* |
| Mixed (one dated, one "today") | Past simple for changes, present simple for current state, in the same paragraph | L1 U2: *The old library building **now contains** laboratories* |

Level 2 Unit 4's answer key gives the reasoning in one line: present perfect "because there is a connection between the past and present." **[M]**

### Introduction

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| The maps show part of the town of Poulton in 1900 and 1935. | ✗ | Mindset ranks this **lowest** — it is lifted straight from the question. |
| The two maps show the changes to X between 1990 and 2010. | B1 | Safe. Paraphrases at least. |
| The maps illustrate how X was modernised between the years 1900 and 1935. **[M]** | B2+ | Adding the *how* clause with a change verb already interprets, which is what the introduction is for. |
| The maps depict an area of the town of Poulton in the years 1900 and 1935 **and the urban developments that took place**. **[M]** | C1 | *Depict* is at home with maps. The appended noun phrase previews the content without listing it. |

### Overview

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Overall, the area changed a lot. | B1 ⚠ | *A lot* is spoken register. Replace with *considerably* or *substantially*. |
| Overall, there were a number of notable changes to the buildings and amenities of this area. **[M]** | B2 | Solid, neutral, quantifies nothing — which is correct for an overview. |
| Generally speaking, the changes illustrate the **extension and improvement** of its facilities during the ten-year period. **[M]** | C1 | Twin nominalisation. This is the model's single best sentence. |
| Overall, the area was transformed **from** a largely industrial district **into** a residential one. | C1 | The *from X into Y* frame is the highest-value map overview move available. It states the whole change in one clause. |

### Change verbs, graded

| Build | Alter | Remove |
|---|---|---|
| **[M]** build, put up *(B1)* | **[M]** change, move *(B1)* | **[M]** knock down, tear down *(B1 ⚠ phrasal, informal-leaning)* |
| **[M]** construct, erect, extend *(B2)* | **[M]** modernise, renovate, relocate *(B2)* | **[M]** demolish, remove, clear *(B2)* |
| **[M]** enlarge, expand, develop *(B2)* | **[M]** convert, replace, alter *(B2)* | **[M]** flatten *(B2)* |
| underwent redevelopment *(C1)* | gave way to *(C1)* | was cleared to make way for *(C1)* |

**Nominalise for Grammatical Range.** The Level 3 model does this throughout: *an **increase** in the number of rooms*, *the **construction** of an extra storey*, *the **relocation** of various facilities*. **[M]** Converting *they built an extra storey* into *the construction of an extra storey* costs nothing and reads two bands higher.

### Prepositions of place **[M]**

*between · opposite · next to · in front of · behind · on the left/right · to the left of · in the middle of · in the top right-hand corner · to the north of · on the outskirts of · adjacent to (C1) · bordering (C1)*

# TYPE 3 — LINE GRAPHS (CHANGE OVER TIME)

*Mindset: L1 U3 (graduate unemployment), L2 U1 (UK housing), L3 U1 (reasons for relocating)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Set by the time marker in the task, not by the task type. See the map below. |
| **Voice** | 85–92% active. The most active-heavy of all Task 1 types. |
| **Tone** | Formal, objective, data-anchored. |
| **Grammar load** | Tense-by-time-marker selection; adverb/adjective pairs; nominalisation; prepositions with figures. |
| **Structure** | Introduction → overview of the trend → grouped body paragraphs → summary with no new data. |

### The tense-by-time-marker map **[M]**

The most valuable single page in the series is Level 3 Unit 1, Exercise 1 — one verb, five time phrases, five tenses.

| Time phrase in the task | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *Since five years ago* | present perfect | sales **have grown** rapidly to 90,000 |
| *Between 1980 and 1990* | past simple | sales **grew** rapidly to 90,000 |
| *From 2020 to 2030* | future | sales **will / are predicted to / are expected to grow** |
| *By the year 2000* | past perfect | sales **had grown** rapidly to 90,000 |
| *By the year 2020* | future perfect | sales **will have grown** rapidly to 90,000 |
| *next year* (Level 2) | *going to* future | house prices **are going to go up** |
| Single-year snapshot, no change | present simple | Greater China and Europe **lead** the way |

One refinement the books model but never state: **the overview often takes present perfect while the body takes past simple**, because the overview treats the period as a whole. Level 1 Unit 3: *Overall, the numbers **have not changed** much… Between 1990 and 2000 there **was** a decrease.* **[M]**

### Introduction

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| The line graph shows the number of X between 1990 and 2015. **[M]** | B1 | Functional floor. |
| The graph illustrates the percentage of X over a twenty-five-year period. | B2 | *Over a … period* beats repeating both dates. |
| The line chart **sets out** the main motivations people expressed for relocating to the countryside. **[M]** | C1 | *Sets out* is the best reporting verb in the series for graphs — precise, formal, unhackneyed. |
| The graph provides a breakdown of X by Y. | C1 | *A breakdown of* is exact and signals you understand the chart's structure. |

### Overview

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| Overall, the numbers increased. | B1 | Technically an overview. Scores the minimum. |
| The overall trend for the period is **of** an increase in… **[M]** | B2+ ⚠ | Mindset's own wording, but *is of an increase* is awkward. Prefer *is one of steady growth* or *was upward*. |
| **The clear trend in the figures is that**… **[M]** | B2+ | A replacement linker the book supplies for *Obviously*. Neutral and data-anchored. |
| **What stands out is that** Greater China and Europe lead the way… **[M]** | C1 | A pseudo-cleft. Foregrounds the key finding grammatically rather than asserting its importance. |
| **It is striking that** the figure fell in every region but one. **[M]** | C1 | Impersonal, formal, and a legitimate way to highlight without editorialising. |

### Movement language, graded

| Verb | Noun | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|---|
| go up / go down | — | B1 | Spoken register. Avoid in writing. |
| rise, fall, increase, decrease **[M]** | a rise, a fall, an increase, a decrease | B2 | The workhorses. |
| drop, decline, dip **[M]** | a drop, a decline, a dip | B2 | *Dip* implies a fall followed by recovery — use it only when the line actually recovers. |
| peak **[M]** | a peak | B2+ | *Peaked at 90,000* is precise and compact. |
| level off, plateau, stabilise | a plateau | B2+ | The one movement candidates consistently fail to name. |
| plummet, rocket **[M]** | — | B2+ ⚠ | Dramatic and journalistic. Level 3 Unit 1 fails a script for magazine tone — reserve these for genuinely extreme movements, once per script. |
| X saw / experienced a sharp rise | — | B2+ | *Witnessed* is overused and faintly personifying; *saw* and *experienced* are safer. |

### Adjective + noun vs adverb + verb **[M]**

| Size | Adjectives (before the noun) | Adverbs (after the verb) |
|---|---|---|
| Big | significant, dramatic, substantial, marked | significantly, dramatically, substantially, considerably, sharply |
| Small | slight, modest, marginal | slightly, moderately, marginally |
| Gradual / none | steady*, stable*, unchanged*, consistent | steadily, gradually, consistently, slowly |
| Fast | rapid, swift | rapidly, quickly |

\* *steady, stable* and *unchanged* usually follow the noun. **[M]**

**Nominalise to lift Grammatical Range.** Level 3 Unit 1's transformation exercise: *increased the most consistently and stably* → ***the most consistent and stable increase was seen in***… **[M]** Same information, a band higher.

### Prepositions with figures **[M]**

*rose **from** 17% **to** 20% · increased **by** 3% · stood **at** 90,000 · a rise **in** the number of · 34% **of** respondents · **between** 7,000 **and** 7,500*

# TYPE 4 — BAR CHARTS AND TABLES

*Mindset: L1 U3 (literacy by region), L2 U1 (house size), L2 U2 (Olympic medals), L3 U5 (UK workforce, car production)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Dated period → past simple. *Of all time* / unfinished period → present perfect. Single-year snapshot → **present simple**. |
| **Voice** | 85–92% active. |
| **Tone** | Formal, comparative, objective. |
| **Grammar load** | Comparatives and their modifiers; *respectively*; grouping and categorising language; referencing to avoid repetition. |
| **Structure** | Introduction → overview → grouped comparisons (never a category-by-category walkthrough) → summary. |

Level 3 Unit 1 fails a sample answer specifically for being "too mechanical — avoid simply listing the changes to a single category." **[M]** Group by pattern, not by row.

### Framing and comparison

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| The bar chart gives the percentage of X. **[M]** | B1 | Fine, flat. |
| **For the purposes of this data set,** the world is divided into seven regions. **[M]** | C1 | A rare and excellent framing move: it explains the chart's structure before describing it. Almost no candidate does this. |
| X and Y **lead the way in terms of** Z, with 26% and 25% **respectively**. **[M]** | B2+ ⚠ | *Respectively* is high-value and underused. *Lead the way* is mildly idiomatic — *account for the largest share* is more neutral. |
| North America **can boast** 19% of production. **[M]** | ⚠ | Printed in the Level 3 model, but *can boast* is evaluative and contradicts the objectivity rule the same book sets in Unit 1. **Do not teach this one.** |
| **Whereas** North America accounts for 19%, South America manufactures just 4%. **[M]** | B2+ | *Whereas* is the cleanest single-sentence contrast available. |
| **By contrast**, very few cars are produced in the Middle East. **[M]** | B2 | Two words, not one — ✗ *bycontrast*, ✗ *in contrast to this* when no contrast exists. |
| **Turning next to** the Americas, / **moving on to**… **[M]** | B2 | Paragraph-hinge markers. One per answer. |
| X is **three times as high as** Y / **more than double** the figure for Y. | B2+ | Multiplicative comparison. Far stronger than *much higher than*. |

### Comparative modifiers **[M]**

*significantly, considerably, substantially, marginally, slightly, far, much* + comparative.
*China has been **significantly more** successful than any other country in badminton.* **[M]**

# TYPE 5 — PIE CHARTS AND PROPORTION

*Mindset: L1 U4 (UK takeaways), L3 U7 (news sources, UK and Brazil)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Usually present simple — a pie chart is a snapshot. Past simple only if the chart is explicitly dated in the past. |
| **Voice** | ~15% passive. The passive earns its place in exactly four jobs (below). |
| **Tone** | Formal, proportional, comparative. |
| **Grammar load** | Fraction/percentage alternation; approximation modifiers; *with* + noun + participle; superlatives. |
| **Structure** | Introduction → overview → the dominant segments → the marginal segments → summary. |

A single pie chart rarely sustains 150 words, so expect two or three, or a pie chart paired with another type. **[M]**

### Expressing proportion

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| 34% of people chose Chinese food. | B1 | Correct; repetitive if used for every segment. |
| **just over a third** / **about a quarter** / **one in ten** / **a tiny proportion** **[M]** | B2 | Alternating fractions with percentages is the core Lexical Resource move for this type. |
| X **accounts for** 26% of the total. | B2+ | The workhorse verb. Safer than the alternatives. |
| X **constitutes** / **makes up** a quarter of respondents. | B2+ / B2 ⚠ | *Constitutes* is the most formal; *makes up* is the most casual of the three but still acceptable. |
| in Brazil the figure is more than half, **at 53%** **[M]** | B2+ | The *, at X%* apposition supplies supporting data without a new clause. Cheap and effective. |
| **with** about two fifths of the population **favouring** this mode of delivery **[M]** | C1 | *With* + noun + present participle. One of the highest-value Task 1 structures in existence and barely taught anywhere. |
| the most popular source is newspapers, **followed in second place by** television **[M]** | B2+ | Passive participle chaining ranks. Compresses two sentences into one. |
| the internet **is more commonly used by** Germans **than** radio **[M]** | B2+ | A passive that **keeps** its agent — deliberately, so *the internet* can occupy subject position. This is foregrounding, and it is the one place a *by*-agent is justified. |

### Approximation modifiers **[M]**

*almost · about · approximately · roughly · just over / just under · a little over / a little under · slightly over / slightly under · between X and Y · around*

### Passive use in data tasks — the four legitimate jobs

1. **Chaining ranks** — *followed in second place by television* **[M]**
2. **Foregrounding the topic** — *the internet is more commonly used by Germans* **[M]**
3. **Locating a superlative** — *The greatest difference is found in South and West Asia* **[M]**
4. **Impersonal hedging in the summary** — *It is apparent that… / In summary, it can be said that…* **[M]**

Level 3 Unit 7's A/B exercise is the only lesson in the series that teaches when **not** to use the passive. Its verdict: the passive wins two cases out of five. **[M]**

| Better | Worse | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The pie chart data **indicate** that… | Social media **is indicated by** the pie chart data to be… | Passive is unnatural; the data is a legitimate active subject. |
| One thing **that can be seen** from the data… | One thing **we can see** from the data… | Passive wins — it removes *we*. |
| Subscriptions **have increased** by 20%. | Subscriptions **have been increased** by 20%. | *Increase* is intransitive here. |
| The data **reveal** that… | **It is revealed by** the data that… | Same fault as row one. |
| The proportion **almost doubled**. | The proportion **was almost doubled**. | *Double* is intransitive here. |

# TYPE 6 — TWO VISUALS IN ONE TASK

*Mindset: L1 U4 (pie chart + line graph), L2 U2 (bar chart + line graph)*

### Profile

| Dimension | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Each visual keeps its own tense, decided by its own time frame. A snapshot pie chart in present simple can sit beside a dated line graph in past simple. |
| **Voice** | Follows whichever type each visual is. |
| **Structure** | Introduction covering **both** → overview covering **both** → one body paragraph per visual → one sentence stating the link between them. |

The introduction must introduce both, the overview must summarise both, and one sentence should explain the **link** between them. **[M]** This last requirement is what separates a Band 7 from a Band 6 on this variant, and most candidates omit it.

| Phrase | Rating | Nuance |
|---|---|---|
| The pie chart shows X, **while** the bar chart shows Y. **[M]** | B2 | The standard dual introduction. Reliable. |
| The first chart gives X; the second **breaks this down by** Y. | B2+ | Names the relationship between the two visuals, not just their contents. |
| **Taken together,** the two charts suggest that… | C1 | The link sentence, done properly. |
| The rise shown in the second chart **corresponds to** the dominance of X in the first. | C1 | Explicit linkage without speculating about cause. *Corresponds to* describes; *is caused by* would breach the no-speculation rule. |

# Where this series leaves gaps

Four things to supply yourself, because Mindset does not:

1. **Plateau language.** No lesson teaches *level off*, *plateau*, *stabilise* or *remain flat*, yet almost every line graph contains a flat segment.
2. **Simultaneity in processes.** Only Level 2 Unit 3 supplies *whilst* and *at the same time*. Students trained on Level 1 alone write every process as linear.
3. **Voice as a choice.** Levels 1 and 2 teach the passive only as a form to drill. The sole lesson on when *not* to use it is Level 3 Unit 7. Stop a student at Level 2 and they will over-passivise.
4. **One internal contradiction.** Level 1 Unit 3's exam rubric instructs *"use the past simple tense,"* but the official sample answer in the answer key is almost entirely **present simple** — correctly, because a single-year literacy chart describes a state, not a change. Teach the model, not the rubric.
